Veediya Display

A typographic exploration drawn from the vernacular streets, storefronts, and temple signs of Kandy, Sri Lanka.

Arts & Culture Typefaces

The starting point: Examining how Sinhala, Tamil, and English sit side-by-side in formal institutional signage along Raja Veediya.

Part One The Audit: Boots on the Ground

A typeface built on assumption is useless. The first step was a massive visual audit of Kandy, tracking exactly how letters function in the wild - from modern streets to colonial architecture.

Part Two Field Research & Categorization

1. Civic & Institutional Structure:Finding the rigid, formal baselines in banks, legal offices, temples, and civic infrastructure.

2. Commerce & Transit:Tracking how brushwork and spacing adapt for maximum legibility on storefronts and moving vehicles.

3. The Unpolished Vernacular:How everyday people handle the complex loops and curves of the script when writing at speed on rough surfaces.

Veediya Display is a typographic case study rooted in visual archaeology. A typeface built on assumption is useless; to design a display face that feels authentically Sri Lankan, I had to isolate the shared visual DNA directly from the source.

I mapped a route through the middle of my hometown - from the Dalada Maligawa to the Municipal Central Market, down Raja Veediya. The goal was to exhaustively document how local sign painters, temple artisans, and everyday shop owners handled the complex loops and proportions of the Sinhala script.

After auditing over 70 unique instances of typography in the wild, I organized the references into three distinct baselines: Institutional Structure (formal colonial-era embossing and civic signs), Commercial Display (high-legibility brushwork on storefronts and lorries), and The Unpolished Vernacular (handwritten price tags, graffiti, and whiteboard menus). The friction between the highly structured civic signs and the raw, high-speed handwriting formed the creative brief for the typeface.

The process doesn't start with a pencil. It starts with a pair of scissors, digitally speaking. Every usable character from the audit gets cropped out of its original photograph and dropped into a raw glyph bank, tagged by the sign it came from. Nothing is redrawn yet - the point is to see the letterforms sitting next to each other exactly as they were found, warts and all.

Once there's a decent bank of extracted letters, I composite them into real words - often mixing a character from a temple plaque with one from a lorry's side panel. Setting found type this way exposes the gaps a single photo can hide: mismatched weight, drifting baselines, stroke contrast that doesn't agree with itself. Wherever the crops fight each other is exactly where the typeface needs a firm decision instead of a borrowed one.

Only after that friction is visible do I start sketching - tracing over the word assemblies to pull out a shared skeleton, x-height, and stroke logic, with a separate pass for the vowel modifiers that carry most of Sinhala's rhythm. Sketches that hold up move into Illustrator for vector tracing, where the tension between street authenticity and kerning-friendly digital legibility actually gets resolved, one anchor point at a time.
